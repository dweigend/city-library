# Kicked Rotator

Research status: complete. Literature research only; implementation acceptance is separate.

NetLogo Kicked Rotator investigates how repeated deterministic kicks produce regular and chaotic angular motion. A single rotor receives momentum increments proportional to the sine of its angle, then advances its angle; kick strength controls the standard-map dynamics. Establishes the resonance-overlap and instability context for the standard map [1]. Analyzes the transition to stochastic behavior through invariant structures [2]. Studies the critical function of the standard map rather than treating all trajectories as equally chaotic [3]. The ideal rotor has impulsive kicks, no friction and no gravitational restoring torque between kicks; interpolation frames affect presentation rather than add physical dynamics. The simulation illustrates coexistence of ordered islands and sensitive trajectories in a simple deterministic map. Reproducing these patterns establishes rule fidelity, not empirical validation.

Registrant abstract consulted: A number of problems in physics can be reduced to the study of a measure-preserving mapping of a plane onto itself. One example is a Hamiltonian system with two degrees of freedom, i.e., two coupled nonlinear oscillators. These are among the simplest deterministic systems that can have chaotic solutions. According to a theorem of Kolmogorov, Arnol’d, and Moser, these systems may also have more ordered orbits lying on curves that divide the plane. The existence of each of these orbit types depends sensitively on both the parameters of the problem and on the initial conditions. The problem addressed in this paper is that of finding when given KAM orbits exist. The guiding hypothesis is that the disappearance of a KAM surface is associated with a sudden change from stability to instability of nearby periodic orbits. The relation between KAM surfaces and periodic orbits has been explored extensively here by the numerical computation of a particular mapping. An important part of this procedure is the introduction of two quantities, the residue and the mean residue, that permit the stability of many orbits to be estimated from the extrapolation of results obtained for a few orbits. The results are distilled into a series of assertions. These are consistent with all that is previously known, strongly supported by numerical results, and lead to a method for deciding the existence of any given KAM surface computationally.
